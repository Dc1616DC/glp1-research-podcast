The mechanisms make sense too - resistant starch ferments in the colon, produces short-chain fatty acids, feeds beneficial bacteria, and can influence satiety hormones. Alex: Why do you think the results were so mixed? Is the Dietary Inflammatory Index not a good measure, or is something else going on?

Dan: The DII has some inherent limitations. It tries to score foods based on their inflammatory potential, but it's based largely on observational data and in vitro studies. Plus, metabolic syndrome in kids is relatively rare, so you need really large sample sizes to detect associations. I'm not sure the inflammatory framework adds much beyond what we already understand about diet quality.

Alex: The carotid intima-media thickness results were even more inconsistent, with only one out of three studies showing an association. That suggests the evidence is pretty weak overall.

Dan: Exactly. And that's important because systematic reviews are supposed to give us the clearest picture of the evidence. When the results are this inconsistent across studies, it suggests either the association is very weak, or there are confounding factors we're not accounting for. Either way, it's not strong enough to base clinical recommendations on.

Alex: Our final study screened 22 different plant foods for their ability to inhibit digestive enzymes - alpha-glucosidase, alpha-amylase, and lipase. The idea being that if you can slow down carb and fat absorption, you might help with metabolic control.

You've got to account for bioavailability, stomach acid, other foods that might interfere, dosing issues - none of that is addressed here. That said, it's not surprising that plants like soybeans and lotus root would have some enzyme inhibitory activity. Plants produce all sorts of compounds to protect themselves, and some of those happen to affect human digestive enzymes.
